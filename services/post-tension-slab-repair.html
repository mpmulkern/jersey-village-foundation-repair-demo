Post-Tension Slab Repair in Jersey Village, TX

Jersey Village Foundation Repair Pros repairs and stabilizes post-tensioned concrete foundations across Jersey Village and the greater Houston metro, starting with a tendon map rather than a shovel. A post-tension slab holds steel cables under roughly 33,000 pounds of tension each, and that single fact changes how every part of the repair has to be planned: where piers can bear, where a hole can be cut, and what a crack across the floor actually means.

Almost every home built in this area since the early 1980s sits on one. The design suits Houston clay well, because a tensioned slab behaves as one stiff plate and rides small ground movements that would crack a conventionally reinforced pour. What it does not do is stop the soil moving. When the clay shrinks away beneath one corner, a post-tension slab still loses support: it simply fails differently, and it needs a different repair.

How Post-Tension Slabs Fail on Houston Clay

The failure pattern here is distinctive. A conventionally reinforced slab tends to crack progressively and in several places as the soil moves, which at least gives a homeowner early warning. A tensioned slab resists, holds its shape across a developing void, and then shows movement suddenly and at the edge: a corner drops, a perimeter crack opens, or a door that closed cleanly for twenty years stops latching within a season.

Three causes account for most of what we document. Clay shrinkage during drought pulls support from beneath the slab edge, leaving the stiff plate cantilevered over nothing. Erosion from roof runoff or a leaking supply line does the same thing faster and more locally. Corrosion at the anchor pockets, usually where an original grout patch failed and let moisture reach the strand end, degrades the tension the design depends on. The third is the one most often missed, because the evidence sits at the slab edge under a flower bed.

Why the Tendon Map Comes First

Cutting a tensioned cable is the one mistake on this type of foundation that cannot be undone cheaply. The strand releases its load instantly, which is a genuine hazard to anyone standing nearby, and the slab permanently loses the compression that section was carrying. The repair then involves a structural engineer, a splice, and a homeowner paying for damage that a scan costing a few hundred dollars would have prevented.

So we map first, without exception, and we hand the marked plan to the homeowner. It stays useful long after our work is finished: the next plumber who needs a drain line, or the contractor pricing a kitchen island with a floor box, starts from a drawing instead of a guess. Any contractor willing to core your slab without scanning it is telling you something useful about how they work.

Post-Tension Repair Versus Conventional Slab Repair

The diagnosis is similar and the execution is not. Both start with an elevation survey, because numbers on a floor grid are the only reliable way to separate a slab that has moved from a floor that was never poured flat. Both usually trace back to soil moisture rather than concrete quality. The difference is constraint: on a conventional slab, a pier goes where the structural load path wants it, while on a tensioned slab it goes where the load path and the cable geometry agree.

That constraint has a cost, and we would rather state it plainly than surprise anyone with it. Expect a post-tension project to carry a scanning line item and a somewhat longer planning stage than the equivalent slab foundation repair. Where readings show real differential drop, the repair is still foundation piering, laid out to the map. Where the slab is close to level but unsupported, void fill is the cheaper and correct answer, and an active water source means drainage correction belongs in the same quote. Homes showing perimeter cracking also need crack repair in the plan, and our elevation survey is what settles which of these applies.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does post-tension slab repair cost in the Houston area?

Tendon location and a documented elevation survey on a post-tension slab generally runs $2,200 to $4,500 as a standalone diagnostic. Piering a post-tensioned foundation typically falls between $9,500 and $21,000 because pier placement has to be mapped around the cable layout before any hole is cut. A large perimeter project with drainage correction included can exceed $24,500. Every figure follows the survey and the tendon map, never a square-foot rule of thumb.

How do I know whether my slab is post-tensioned?

Most Houston-area homes built from the early 1980s onward are post-tensioned, and many carry a stamped or cast warning plate near the garage door or an exterior corner reading that the slab contains post-tensioned cables and must not be cut or drilled. Sealed pockets spaced along the slab edge, roughly the size of a fist and patched with grout, are the other visible sign. Where no plate survives, we confirm with a scan before any work is planned.

Can a broken tendon be repaired, or does the slab need replacing?

A broken tendon is repairable in the great majority of cases, and slab replacement is very rarely the honest answer. A structural engineer specifies either a coupled splice on the existing strand or a replacement anchor at the edge, depending on where the break sits and how much of the cable is sound. What matters more is the cause: a tendon that failed because the slab was left unsupported over a void will break again if only the cable is addressed.

Is it safe to pier a post-tensioned slab?

Yes, and it is routine work, provided the cable layout is mapped first. Piers bear on brackets fixed to the slab edge or on interior points established clear of the tendons, so the strands are never cut. The risk on a post-tension slab is not piering itself: it is drilling or coring blind. A cut tendon releases its load instantly, which is both a safety hazard to the crew and a structural loss to the slab.

Do you scan before drilling for plumbing or a doorway?

Always, and we will decline to core a post-tensioned slab that has not been scanned. Ground-penetrating radar and electromagnetic location put the tendon positions on a marked floor plan before anyone starts a hole. The scan is inexpensive against the cost of a severed cable, and we provide the marked plan to the plumber or remodeller who needs it rather than keeping it to ourselves.

Warranty on Post-Tension Work

Piering installed on a post-tensioned foundation carries the same lifetime transferable warranty as our conventional piering, because the piers reach strata below the seasonal moisture zone and their performance does not depend on the cables. Tendon splice and anchor repairs carry a ten-year workmanship warranty on the repair itself. Scanning is warranted for accuracy within the marked area, which is why we mark the floor rather than describing cable positions verbally.
