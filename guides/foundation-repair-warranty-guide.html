Foundation Repair Warranties in Houston: What They Actually Cover

Quick Answer

A lifetime foundation repair warranty in Houston almost always covers the piers that contractor installed, at those locations, for as long as you own the home. It rarely covers new movement in unpiered areas, cosmetic repairs after an adjustment, or damage from drainage and plumbing faults. The two questions that decide whether a warranty is worth anything are whether it transfers to the next owner and whether it survives the contractor going out of business.

Foundation repair in the Houston area runs from roughly $2,200 for crack work to well over $24,500 for a full structural project, and the warranty is a substantial part of what you are buying. It is also the part almost nobody reads carefully. Two contractors can both advertise a lifetime transferable warranty and offer materially different protection once you compare the actual documents.

This guide explains what the standard terms mean in practice, which exclusions appear in nearly every contract, and what to verify before signing. It applies to foundation work across Jersey Village, Houston, Cypress, Spring, Katy, and the surrounding metro, where expansive clay soil guarantees the question of future movement will come up eventually.

What Lifetime Actually Means

Lifetime in this industry means the lifetime of the structure, or in most contracts the duration of your ownership of it, and it applies to specific installed work rather than to the foundation as a whole. If a contractor drives twelve piers along your north perimeter and one of them settles beyond the contract tolerance in year nine, they return and adjust it at no charge for the pier work.

The important word is "installed". If your south perimeter starts moving in year nine, that is new movement in an area nobody piered, and it is a new project at full price. This is not a loophole or bad faith: no contractor can warranty soil they never worked on. But homeowners routinely read lifetime warranty as whole-house insurance against any future foundation problem, and that is not what any contract in this market says.

Transferable Versus Non-Transferable

A transferable warranty passes to the next owner when you sell. A non-transferable one ends at closing. That difference is worth real money in the Houston resale market.

Disclosed foundation repair with a live transferable warranty tends to reassure a buyer: the problem was identified, corrected professionally, and someone remains on the hook for it. The same repair with a dead warranty reads as an unquantified risk, and buyers price that risk conservatively. Sellers regularly find the gap between the two situations exceeds what the transferable option would have cost at the time of the work.

Read the transfer clause rather than the headline. Common restrictions include a transfer fee of $100 to $500, a requirement to register the transfer within a set window after closing, and a limit of one transfer over the warranty's life. None of these are unreasonable, but they are the kind of detail that causes trouble at the worst possible moment if you discover them during a sale.

The Exclusions That Appear in Almost Every Contract

  • Movement in areas of the foundation that were not piered under the original contract
  • Damage caused by plumbing leaks beneath or around the slab
  • Damage arising from drainage the homeowner was told to correct and did not
  • Cosmetic restoration such as drywall, tile, paint, and flooring after a warranty adjustment
  • Any work another contractor performed on the same foundation afterward
  • Movement following major site changes: a new pool, a large addition, or significant excavation
  • Damage from named catastrophic events, which is an insurance matter rather than a warranty one

The cosmetic exclusion surprises people most often. If a warranty adjustment lifts a pier back to spec, that movement can crack drywall and tile again, and repairing those finishes is usually the homeowner's cost. Ask specifically how a contractor handles cosmetic damage during warranty work, because a minority do include a finish allowance and it is rarely advertised.

Service Fees and Annual Inspection Requirements

Two structures quietly reduce the value of an otherwise strong warranty. The first is a mandatory paid annual inspection: miss one, and coverage can lapse permanently. Over a twenty-year period at a typical inspection price, that is a meaningful running cost attached to something sold as free lifetime protection.

The second is a per-claim service fee, often $100 to $350, charged each time you invoke the warranty. Neither practice is improper when it is disclosed clearly. Both are worth finding in the document before you sign, because they turn "lifetime warranty included" into an ongoing obligation you did not budget for.

Third-Party Backing: The Risk Nobody Mentions

A standard contractor warranty is a commitment from that specific business. If the company closes, sells, or reorganises under a new name, the commitment generally goes with it. In an industry with meaningful turnover, a twenty-five-year warranty from a company two years old is a weaker instrument than a ten-year warranty from a firm that has traded locally for two decades.

Third-party backed warranties address this by insuring the obligation through a separate underwriter, so coverage survives the contractor's closure. They are less common and usually cost more. Whether you need one depends on the contractor: ask how long they have operated under the current name, whether the licence and insurance are current in Texas, and how many warranty claims they serviced in the past year. A vague answer to that last question tells you something useful.

Warranties on Non-Piering Work

Piering carries the longest warranties because piers reach load-bearing strata below the zone where seasonal moisture changes anything. Other repairs sit in soil that moves every year, so their terms are legitimately shorter. Expect roughly five years on slab void fill, one to five years on crack repair depending on method, and one to three years on drainage correction where the work is partly dependent on maintenance you control.

A contractor offering a lifetime warranty on drainage work is worth a second look. Drainage performance depends on gutters staying clear, grading being maintained, and landscaping not being changed, none of which the contractor controls. A shorter, honest term on that line item is a better sign than an implausibly long one.

What to Ask Before You Sign

  • Is the warranty transferable, and what fee or deadline applies at transfer?
  • Does it cover only the piered locations, or any part of the foundation?
  • What settlement tolerance triggers a warranty adjustment, stated as a measurement?
  • Is there a service fee per claim, and is an annual inspection required to keep coverage alive?
  • Is the warranty third-party backed, and by which underwriter?
  • Are cosmetic finishes covered after a warranty adjustment, and to what limit?
  • How long has the company traded under its current name, and how many claims did it service last year?

Ask for the warranty document itself, not the summary in the proposal. A contractor who is comfortable handing over the full terms before you commit is generally comfortable honouring them later.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a lifetime foundation repair warranty actually cover?

In nearly every case it covers the piers the contractor installed, at the locations they installed them, for as long as you own the home. If a warrantied pier settles beyond the stated tolerance, the contractor returns and adjusts it. What it almost never covers is new movement elsewhere on the foundation, cosmetic repairs after the adjustment, or damage from drainage and plumbing problems. Lifetime describes the duration of coverage on specific work, not blanket protection for the whole house.

Is a transferable warranty worth paying more for?

Usually yes, particularly if there is any chance you sell within a decade. A transferable warranty moves to the next owner and turns documented foundation repair from a negotiating liability into a selling point. Buyers reliably discount a house with repaired-but-unwarrantied movement. Check the transfer terms though: some contractors charge a transfer fee, and some limit the transfer to a single owner change.

What is typically excluded from a foundation repair warranty?

The standard exclusions are movement in unpiered areas of the foundation, damage from plumbing leaks, damage from drainage the homeowner did not maintain, cosmetic repairs such as drywall and tile after a warranty adjustment, and any work performed by another contractor on the same foundation. Several contracts also void coverage if the owner adds a pool, a large addition, or major landscaping near the repaired perimeter.

Do foundation warranties have annual fees or inspection requirements?

Some do. There are Houston contractors whose warranties require a paid annual inspection to remain valid, and missing one can void the coverage entirely. Others charge a service call fee each time you make a claim. Neither arrangement is dishonest as long as it is disclosed, but both change the real value of the warranty and are worth finding in the contract before you sign rather than after.

How do I check whether a foundation warranty is actually worth anything?

A warranty is only as good as the company standing behind it. Check how long the contractor has traded under its current name, whether it is licensed and insured in Texas, and whether it carries third-party warranty backing that survives the company closing. Ask directly how many warranty claims they serviced last year. A contractor with an established local history and a straight answer to that question is offering something real.

Does a foundation warranty transfer if the contractor goes out of business?

Not unless it is third-party backed. A standard contractor warranty is a promise from that specific company, and it ends when the company does. This is the single largest risk in a foundation warranty and the reason the length of a contractor's local track record matters more than the length printed on the certificate. Third-party backed warranties are insured through a separate underwriter and survive the contractor's closure.
