Why Spring Foundations Move

The clay beneath Spring behaves the same way it does across the Houston metro: it takes on water and swells, then releases it and shrinks. Several inches of vertical movement between a wet spring and a drought summer is unremarkable here. A slab handles that fine as long as the whole footprint moves together. Damage happens when one part of the house sits over soil in a different moisture state than another, and the concrete has to bridge the difference.

In Spring, tree cover is the leading cause of that imbalance. A mature pine or oak within twenty feet of the house can pull dozens of gallons a day out of the soil during summer, drying the clay on that side of the slab while the shaded or irrigated side holds moisture. Age compounds it: a home built in 1978 has now been through more than forty full cycles, and the accumulated movement from each one is what eventually opens a crack that will not close again.

Root Barriers Before Piers

On Spring properties where trees are driving the movement, we often recommend starting with a root barrier and a watering plan rather than structural work. A barrier is a vertical membrane set between the tree and the foundation, deep enough to stop roots from reaching under the slab edge. It costs a fraction of a piering project and, where the elevation survey shows only modest differential, it frequently stabilises the house without any structural repair at all.

That is not always the right call. Where readings show a real drop at a load-bearing line, or where a season of monitoring shows the pattern still worsening, piers are the honest answer and delaying only adds damage. The point is that the survey decides, and a contractor who quotes piers before measuring anything is not giving you a diagnosis. Details are on our drainage correction and foundation piering pages.

Repairs That Hold in Spring Conditions

Piering restores elevation and stops a settled foundation from dropping further, but on clay soil the repair holds best when the moisture conditions that caused the movement are addressed alongside it. We quote drainage or root-barrier work with structural repair whenever the assessment supports it, and we separate the two on the estimate so you can see which portion is the structural fix and which is prevention. Doing both at once also avoids excavating and restoring the same landscaping twice.

Homes with unsupported slab sections are covered under slab void fill, and older pier and beam construction around Old Town Spring falls under pier and beam leveling. If you are unsure which applies, our free elevation survey establishes it with recorded numbers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do so many Spring homes have foundation movement?

Spring sits on the same expansive clay belt as the rest of north Harris County, and much of its housing stock dates from rapid development in the 1970s and 1980s. Those homes have now been through forty-plus cycles of wet and dry seasons, and many sit among mature pines and oaks whose root systems draw significant moisture from beneath the slab edge. Age plus tree cover is a common combination here.

Do the trees in Spring neighborhoods cause foundation problems?

Mature trees are one of the most frequent contributing factors we document in Spring. A large tree can draw dozens of gallons of water a day from the soil, and if the canopy sits close to the house, it dries the clay on that side while the opposite side stays at normal moisture. The resulting differential is what moves the slab. A root barrier usually addresses it without removing the tree.

Is foundation repair in Spring covered by homeowners insurance?

Usually not. Standard Texas policies exclude settlement caused by soil movement, which covers the large majority of the repairs we perform. The exception is damage from a sudden covered event, such as a plumbing leak under the slab washing out soil. If that is a possibility on your home, we document the evidence during the inspection so you can take it to your carrier.
